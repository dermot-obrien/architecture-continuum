# Architecture assessment on the continuum

How designs are assessed in current practice, what TOGAF says about it, what changes when a design uses AI models or AI agents, where architecture assessment ends and cybersecurity assessment begins, and what the bundle should do about each. The research was done between 27 and 28 October 2026 from primary sources where they could be reached; the [caveats](#caveats) say where they could not. The proposal it leads to is drafted as the [Architecture Assessment Standard](../standards/assessment/standard-architecture-assessment.md), with a schema for a design written as data, four lenses of criteria (one of them for the formal assessment of a solution before it goes into production), a worked example, and a [guide for the design's author](design-author-guide.md).

## In brief

1. Assessment is a family of judgements, and the continuum already makes two of them: the definition stage (`/architecture stage`) and catalogue conformance (`/architecture check`). What it lacks is the judgement of fitness for purpose: does this design meet its stakeholders' concerns and quality attributes, and what does it trade? ISO/IEC/IEEE 42030 names that judgement, ISO/IEC/IEEE 42020 gives it a process, and ATAM is its best-known method.
2. TOGAF 10's assessment instruments are governance instruments. The compliance review, its six levels of conformance, the Architecture Board and the Architecture Contract check an implementation against an architecture. They do not evaluate whether the architecture is any good. TOGAF has techniques for that (gap analysis, trade-offs, risk with initial and residual levels, capability and readiness assessment) but no evaluation method. Technical Corrigendum 1 (May 2025) took the compliance checklists out of the standard; they were revised and published separately as G253 (December 2025). The maturity models now sit in Series Guide G203.
3. Practice has moved from boards and gates to continuous, team-run and automated assessment: fitness functions, policy and architecture rules as code, service scorecards, the architecture advice process, and lightweight team reviews such as LASR. DORA's evidence that heavyweight external approval does not reduce change failure is why. The vendors moved too: in 2026 AWS previewed an agent that runs Well-Architected reviews continuously, and Google publishes its framework as agent skills.
4. The unit of assessment differs by tradition, and the continuum can name each one: the entity of interest (42030), a system architecture (ATAM), a workload (the Well-Architected frameworks, which on the continuum is what a product runs), a platform (the CNCF maturity model), a team or service (DORA). A design on the continuum is an architecture pattern, a reference architecture or a solution design, and each is assessable.
5. AI solutions add quality attributes the classic models lack (non-determinism, confabulation, drift, evaluation coverage, cost per call, controllability) and make evaluations the main evidence. ISO/IEC 25059:2023 extends the 25010 quality model for AI; NIST AI 600-1, the OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications 2025 and the cloud vendors' AI lenses give the criteria; the EU AI Act's Annex IV is the closest thing in law to an architecture evidence pack.
6. AI agents add one question above all: what can the agent do in the world, and who or what bounds it? The OWASP Top 10 for Agentic Applications (December 2025), the AWS Agentic AI Lens (June 2026), Singapore's agentic governance framework (version 1.5, May 2026), Meta's Agents Rule of Two and the design patterns for resisting prompt injection all converge on the same checks: an inventory of actions with their reversibility, limits enforced outside the agent, no single session that combines untrusted input, sensitive data and consequential action, an identity of its own, and evaluation of trajectories rather than outcomes. The bundle's agent-native standards already hold most of the mechanisms (tiers T0 to T3, capability scope, provenance, reconciliation); what they lack is the assessment that applies them to a design.
7. Architecture assessment and cybersecurity assessment overlap on threat modelling and nowhere else. Architecture assesses a design across all concerns, without accreditation; cybersecurity assesses whether a system's controls are implemented and effective and whether residual risk is acceptable, often by an accredited and independent assessor. The architecture assessment should own security architecture, require a threat model, and hand the rest over.
8. The recommendation is one general method with lenses: a design written as data against a schema, typed by continuum kinds; a core lens for every design; AI solution and AI agent lenses brought in by what the design contains; an assessment record whose findings go to the continuum's existing kinds (consideration, decision record, technical debt); and three depths (check, review, evaluation). It is drafted in `standards/assessment/`, and it is the contract for a future `/architecture assess` flow.

## What assessment means here

The word covers judgements that answer different questions, and keeping them apart is the first job.

| Judgement | Question | In the bundle today | In practice |
|---|---|---|---|
| Definition | How far has a capability been defined and built? | `/architecture stage`, the definition ladder R0 to R6 | No industry equivalent; capability maturity is a different axis |
| Conformance | Does the artefact follow the standards? | `/architecture check`, mechanical, over the catalogue | TOGAF compliance review; Structurizr inspections; ArchUnit; OPA |
| Fitness for purpose | Does the design meet its concerns and quality attributes, and what does it trade? | Not present | ISO/IEC/IEEE 42030; ATAM and its lightweight variants; Well-Architected reviews |
| Maturity | How well does a capability, platform or practice perform? | Capability `maturity`, 0 to 5 | TOGAF G203 (architecture maturity); BIZBOK heat maps; CNCF platform maturity; CMMI v3.0; DORA |
| Readiness | Can the organisation make the change? | Not present | TOGAF Business Transformation Readiness Assessment; G212 digital readiness; The Open Group W260 AI adoption capabilities |
| Security | Is security risk treated and are controls effective? | Agent-native guardrails, for agents only | NIST SP 800-30, 800-53A and 800-37; ISO/IEC 27001 and 27005; IRAP, CAF, FedRAMP |
| Regulatory conformity | Does the system meet an instrument's requirements? | Not present | EU AI Act conformity assessment; ISO/IEC 42001 certification; NIS2, CRA, DORA |

The bundle's gap is the third row. The other rows either exist already or belong to other practices, and the right relationship with them is a hand-over, not absorption.

## Architecture assessment and cybersecurity assessment

The two are often run by the same review board and confused for that reason. They differ in their question, their object, who may perform them and what they produce.

| | Architecture assessment | Cybersecurity assessment |
|---|---|---|
| Purpose | Judge whether a design is fit for purpose across all its stakeholders' concerns, and expose its trade-offs and risks to the decisions that remain | Establish whether security risk is understood and acceptably treated, and whether controls are in place and effective, usually to support a risk-acceptance or compliance decision |
| Question | Does this design meet its concerns, and what does it trade? (42030, ATAM) | What can go wrong, are the controls implemented and effective, and is the residual risk acceptable? (NIST SP 800-30, 800-53A, 800-37) |
| Object | The design: an architecture description, its scenarios and decisions | The system: its authorisation or system boundary, configuration, controls, tests and logs |
| Unit | A design on the continuum; a workload in Well-Architected terms | An authorisation boundary (RMF, IRAP, NZISM), an ISMS scope (ISO/IEC 27001), a service organisation's system (SOC 2), a product (EU CRA, EUCC), an entity (NIS2, NCSC CAF), a cloud offering (FedRAMP) |
| Standards | ISO/IEC/IEEE 42010, 42020 and 42030; ISO/IEC 25010 and 25059; ATAM; TOGAF; the Well-Architected frameworks | NIST SP 800-30, 800-53A Rev. 5 (release 5.2.0, August 2025), 800-37 and 800-115; NIST CSF 2.0; ISO/IEC 27001:2022 and 27005:2022; CIS Controls v8.1; OWASP ASVS 5.0.0 and WSTG; SSDF, SAMM, BSIMM; SLSA; ISM and IRAP; NZISM; CAF and Cyber Essentials; NIS2, CRA and DORA; EUCC; FedRAMP 20x |
| Assessor | Architects and stakeholders; no accreditation regime | Often independent and accredited: an IRAP assessor endorsed by ASD, a FedRAMP 3PAO accredited to ISO/IEC 17020, a certification body, a licensed CPA for SOC 2 |
| Evidence | Specifications, views, scenarios, decision records, evals, stakeholder input | Configurations, test results, scans, interviews, logs, policies, machine-validated indicators |
| Cadence | At design decisions, stage gates and significant change | Periodic audits, continuous monitoring, threat-led tests every three years under DORA, regulatory deadlines |
| Outputs | Findings, risks, sensitivity and trade-off points, decisions, a verdict | An authorisation to operate, a security assessment report, a plan of action and milestones, a risk register, certificates, maturity ratings, test findings |

The overlap is real but narrow. Threat modelling, in the Threat Modeling Manifesto's words, is "analyzing representations of a system to surface concerns about security and privacy characteristics", and a representation of a system is what an architecture assessment reads. Security architecture review, NIST SP 800-160's systems security engineering, SABSA, The Open Group's G152 on integrating risk and security into TOGAF, zero trust design (NIST SP 800-207) and the security pillar of each Well-Architected framework all sit in the overlap. A penetration test, a control assessment, an ISMS audit and an authorisation decision do not.

The proposed rule, written into the [standard](../standards/assessment/standard-architecture-assessment.md#9-the-line-with-cybersecurity-assessment), is that the architecture assessment owns security architecture (trust zones, authentication and authorisation across them, identities, where untrusted content enters and what it can reach, where controls are placed), requires a threat model for the version it assesses, and hands everything else to the cybersecurity assessment, whose outcome then becomes evidence the design cites. The same rule applies to privacy impact, AI impact and regulatory conformity assessments: architecture names the obligation and how the design classes under it, and hands the judgement over.

AI does not move the line. AI red teaming, ISO/IEC 27090 (security threats to AI systems, reported published in October 2026), ETSI EN 304 223 (baseline security requirements for AI, January 2026) and NIST's control overlays for AI systems (COSAiS) are on the cybersecurity side: control baselines and adversarial tests. The architectural choices that decide how much an AI system is exposed (what a model can read, what its output can reach, what an agent can do unaided) stay on the architecture side, which is why the agent lens below carries so many security-shaped criteria.

## What the standards say

Three ISO/IEC/IEEE standards from JTC 1/SC 7 divide the ground, and the continuum already cites the first.

| Standard | Covers | What it gives an assessment |
|---|---|---|
| ISO/IEC/IEEE 42010:2022 | Architecture description: stakeholders, concerns, viewpoints, views, model kinds, correspondences, decisions and rationale; "entity of interest" in place of "system of interest" | The conformance baseline for a description, as distinct from the architecture it describes |
| ISO/IEC/IEEE 42020:2019 (a second edition is in development) | Architecture processes: governance, management, conceptualisation, evaluation, elaboration and enablement | Clause 9, the architecture evaluation process: prepare; determine objectives and criteria; choose methods; establish measures; collect; analyse and assess stakeholder value; characterise; formulate findings and recommendations; communicate |
| ISO/IEC/IEEE 42030:2019 | Architecture evaluation framework | The concepts: an evaluation has objectives, factors, approaches and results; analysis establishes properties, value assessment applies stakeholder judgement; an organisation derives its own framework from the generic one (ATAM is an example); the outputs are an evaluation plan and an evaluation report. A revision was registered as a new work item in May 2026 |
| ISO/IEC 25010:2023 | Product quality model, with 25002 and 25019 | Nine characteristics: functional suitability, performance efficiency, compatibility, interaction capability (was usability), reliability, security, maintainability, flexibility (was portability), and the new safety. The vocabulary of every quality scenario |
| ISO/IEC 25059:2023 | Quality model for AI systems | Adds user controllability, functional adaptability, robustness, transparency and intervenability, and modifies functional correctness. Marked for revision |

The bundle's proposed [standard](../standards/assessment/standard-architecture-assessment.md) is, in 42030's terms, a customised evaluation framework: its lenses are the evaluation factors, its depths the approaches, its assessment record the evaluation report.

## TOGAF

The TOGAF Standard, 10th Edition (C220, April 2022; Technical Corrigendum 1 applied May 2025) is modular: six documents of fundamental content and 26 Series Guides. Its assessment material is spread across them, and most of it is governance. The corrigendum matters to anyone citing chapter numbers: besides removing the compliance checklists, it moved the migration planning techniques out of ADM Techniques into Architecture Content, so every later ADM Techniques chapter moved up by one. The numbers below are the corrected ones.

| Instrument | Where in TOGAF 10 | What it assesses | Lands on the continuum as |
|---|---|---|---|
| Architecture compliance review | Enterprise Architecture Capability and Governance, chapter 6: a process of twelve steps (6.4.3), from the request for a review to the lead architect sending the report to the review co-ordinator, with eight roles (6.4.2): Architecture Board, project leader, review co-ordinator, lead enterprise architect, architect, customer, business domain expert and project principals. ADM Phase G step 11.3.4, "Perform Enterprise Architecture Compliance Reviews" | Whether an implementation project conforms to the architecture, where "in accordance with" means supporting the stated strategy, adhering to the stated standards and principles, and providing the stated functionality | The verdict's conformance level; `/architecture check` for the catalogue |
| Levels of conformance | Same chapter, 6.2, figure 6-1; the wording of "conformant" was corrected by Technical Corrigendum 1, because the earlier text made it read like non-conformant | Irrelevant, consistent, compliant, conformant, fully conformant, non-conformant, in that order: how far the features of a specification and an implementation coincide, and whether those they share are in accordance | Adopted as the conformance level of a verdict, with the design specification in the place of the implementation |
| Compliance checklists | No longer in the standard: Technical Corrigendum 1 removed them from chapter 6, which now only refers to G253, Architecture Compliance Review Checklists (December 2025), a revised guide to be used in design reviews, governance gates and solution assessments | Eleven sets of questions: implementation governance; stakeholder and general architecture; security architecture; novel technology; compute platform, network and end-user device; applications; application integration; information management; infrastructure service; system management; system engineering and cross-domain architecture | Questions the core lens mostly covers as criteria a specification can answer; the gaps are listed [below](#g253-against-the-core-lens) |
| Governance Repository | Architecture Content, chapter 7, 7.5 | Where governance results are kept: a decision log, compliance assessments for each project (overview, progress, completed checklists, the standards compliance assessment and recommended actions), capability assessments, a calendar of reviews, the project portfolio, and performance measurement | The assessment record kept beside its design, and the decision records its dispositions open |
| Architecture Board | Capability and Governance, chapter 4 | Who sponsors and rules on compliance and dispensations | The lead assessor and the owner who accepts risks; the advice process in place of a gate |
| Architecture Contract | Capability and Governance, chapter 5 | The agreement between the architecture function and those who deliver or sponsor it | The accepted design specification and its decision records |
| Architecture maturity models | Series Guide G203 (April 2022), moved out of the core; its example framework is the US Department of Commerce Architecture Capability Maturity Model, with CMMI alongside | The maturity of an organisation's architecture practice | Not a design assessment; out of scope here |
| Business Transformation Readiness Assessment | ADM Techniques, chapter 7; Phase A | Readiness factors, with an example set of twelve from the Canadian Government's Business Transformation Enablement Program. Each factor is given a baseline and a target state, then rated for urgency, readiness status (low, fair, acceptable, good or high) and difficulty to fix | A readiness lens, possible later |
| Capability assessment | ADM Phase A, steps 3.3.4 "Evaluate Capabilities" and 3.3.5 "Assess Readiness for Business Transformation"; the Capability Assessment deliverable | Baseline and target capability, and the capability to change | The capability's maturity and its stage |
| Gap analysis | ADM Techniques, chapter 5 | Shortfalls between baseline and target building blocks, "to validate an architecture that is being developed" | Considerations, and the `lifecycle_state` of building blocks |
| Risk management | ADM Techniques, chapter 8 | An initial level of risk before mitigation and a residual level after it, each found by combining effect (catastrophic, critical, marginal, negligible) with frequency (frequent, likely, occasional, seldom, unlikely) into an impact of extremely high, high, moderate or low. Residual risk is accepted through governance and monitored in Phase G | The design's `risks`, which carry one likelihood and impact and do not yet separate initial from residual; `accept-risk` dispositions |
| Architecture principles | ADM Techniques, chapter 2 | Each principle's statement, rationale and implications | Constraints a review checks against |
| Trade-offs | ADM Techniques, chapter 9 | Alternatives and their trade-offs | Decision records with alternatives; trade-off points |

Four observations shape the proposal. First, TOGAF's compliance review asks whether an implementation follows an architecture, and its levels describe a relationship between two specifications; it says nothing about whether the architecture itself is fit for purpose, which is exactly the judgement the continuum lacks. Second, the levels are still useful as a conformance scale when the "architecture" is the continuum's standards and the "implementation" is a design, and the draft standard adopts them that way. Third, The Open Group itself has moved governance towards guardrails: the Enabling Enterprise Agility guide (G20F) and Applying the ADM using Agile Sprints (G210) recast principles and policies as guardrails, the Open Agile Architecture standard (version 2.0, C262, April 2026) adopts fitness functions as a way to "objectively assess whether the system is actually meeting its identified non-functional requirements" and treats guardrails as lightweight governance, under which a team that follows them needs no approval and a team that departs from them must make its case (chapter 11, sections 11.3.2 and 11.3.3), and the Digital Practitioner Body of Knowledge argues for governance enforced in the delivery pipeline and warns that checkpoints add risk. Fourth, TOGAF's compliance review is a judgement of alignment to the target architecture, and the continuum already marks every artefact as baseline, in flight, target or retired; the core lens therefore asks, in its target-state area, whether a design moves its capabilities toward the target and records every departure from it, and the verdict's conformance level is judged against the target state rather than against the standards alone.

On AI, The Open Group has published a white paper on AI adoption capabilities for enterprise architects (W260, April 2026), a guide to AI in digital transformation (G268, May 2026) and earlier business-leader material, but no assessment method for AI systems and nothing on agents that could be found in its public catalogue. W260 assesses an organisation's readiness, not a design ([below](#w260-against-the-ai-solution-lens)). ArchiMate 4 (C260, April 2026) removed the gap and contract elements, which matters to anyone who modelled gap analysis or Architecture Contracts in ArchiMate. The critiques are well known: Kotusev argues the ADM works "more as a symbol than as a realistic actionable guidance", and the lighter alternatives (minimum viable architecture, the advice process, fitness functions) are where most teams now are.

### G253 against the core lens

G253's questions were compared, set by set, with the [`core` lens](../standards/assessment/lenses/core.yaml). Its two governance sets (implementation governance; stakeholder and general architecture) ask about the review itself, not the design: whether the target was reasonably interpreted, whether experts agree with the impact assessment, and whether stakeholders approved enforcing the target, granting relief or changing it. The standard's process, findings, dispositions and verdict answer those. Questions about whether controls are operated (security monitoring, incident response, awareness training, network controls, patching, multi-factor authentication) are on the cybersecurity side of the line in [section 9](../standards/assessment/standard-architecture-assessment.md#9-the-line-with-cybersecurity-assessment) of the standard and are handed over, not gaps.

| G253 set | Covered by | Not covered |
|---|---|---|
| Security architecture | `CORE-SEC-01` to `05` (identity, authentication and authorisation across trust zones, external access, threat model), `CORE-DAT-01` and `02` (sensitive data) | What the audit trail records and who may alter it; security responsibilities held by third parties |
| Novel technology | Largely organisational, W260's ground | Whether technology new to the enterprise has had its maturity and feasibility assessed |
| Compute platform, network and end-user device | `CORE-STR-07` (platform dependencies), `CORE-CST-01` (running cost), `CORE-TGT-01` and `02` (non-standard choices recorded as departures) | Whole-life cost; the supplier's financial standing |
| Applications | `CORE-TRC-01` (catalogued), `CORE-RSK-02` (privacy and reporting obligations), `CORE-DAT-02` (retention), `CORE-OPS-03` (recovery) | Nothing at the level of a design |
| Application integration | `CORE-INT-01` to `03` | Nothing |
| Information management | `CORE-DAT-01` to `03` (owner, classification, residency, retention, lineage, one writer) | Data quality requirements |
| Infrastructure service | `CORE-QA-01` to `04` (availability, scaling), `CORE-OPS-02` and `03` (monitoring, recovery), `CORE-CST-01`, `CORE-TGT-02` (a need the standard services do not meet, recorded as a departure) | Nothing at the level of a design |
| System management | `CORE-OPS-02` to `04` (observability, deployment and rollback, backup and recovery objectives, runbook) | Licence management; how the system is decommissioned |
| System engineering and cross-domain architecture | `CORE-INT` (integration), `CORE-QA` and `CORE-OPS-01` (users, load, performance, up-time), `CORE-OPS-03` (redeployment) | A defined life expectancy; vendor stability, roadmap and exit for packages; usability, accessibility, internationalisation and localisation |

Six gaps follow. The first is now closed: the core lens has a target-state area (`CORE-TGT-01` to `05`), which records a design's departures from the target state and its standards as decisions (dispensations, in TOGAF's word), time-bound ones with an expiry and a path back, and asks that a target the design shows to be wrong be raised for change rather than worked around. The other five remain candidates for a later version: whole-life cost and life expectancy, including licences and decommissioning, beside the running cost `CORE-CST-01` asks for; supplier and package viability, with an exit; maturity and feasibility of technology new to the enterprise; interaction capability (usability, accessibility, internationalisation), which ISO/IEC 25010 names but `CORE-QA-02` does not list; and data quality requirements together with the design of the audit trail.

### W260 against the AI solution lens

W260 is a reference architecture of the organisational capabilities that adopting AI depends on, laid out for three periods (developing the path forward, pursuing the transformation, operating afterwards) and a foundation common to all three, with properties for rating how far each capability is needed (agility, competency, maturity on CMMI's levels, operating model, performance state, performer). It is a readiness instrument and belongs with the readiness row of the table at the top, not with design criteria. A few of its capabilities do turn into questions a design can answer, and against the [`ai-solution` lens](../standards/assessment/lenses/ai-solution.yaml) they fall out as follows.

| W260 capability | Design question | In the lens |
|---|---|---|
| Identify legitimate information use | Is the data sent to a model at run time, including to a third-party provider, permitted for that use? | Not covered: `AIS-DATA-04` covers training, fine-tuning and evaluation data, and `AIS-DATA-02` the caller's permissions, not purpose |
| Understand AI contract requirements; draft AI elements in contract | Are the provider's terms on data use and retention, rights in outputs, and its failure or acquisition recorded against the design? | Partly: `AIS-MOD-03` asks for replaceability and a fallback, not for the terms |
| Experiment; scale experiment | Does the design say whether it is an experiment, with its boundary and success criteria, and what must change before it runs at scale? | Not covered |
| Maintain AI instruction | When the provider changes or retires a model, is the design revalidated before the change is used? | Covered by `AIS-MOD-01` and `AIS-EVAL-02` |
| Modular development; technology lifecycle | Can the model and its platform be replaced and retired without the rest? | Covered by `AIS-MOD-03` |
| IT operations (observability) | Is the quality of service of a non-deterministic system observed? | Covered by `AIS-OPS-02`, `AIS-OPS-03` and `AIS-DATA-03` |

The first three are candidate criteria for the next version of the AI solution lens.

## Contemporary practice beyond TOGAF

### Scenario-based evaluation

The SEI's Architecture Tradeoff Analysis Method remains the reference: business drivers, a utility tree that refines quality attributes into prioritised scenarios rated on importance and difficulty, analysis of the architectural approaches against them, and as outputs the risks, non-risks, sensitivity points, trade-off points and risk themes linked to the drivers. It is costly (an external team, three to four days), and studies of industrial practice find it rarely used. Its family includes the Quality Attribute Workshop and Mini-QAW for eliciting scenarios before there is an architecture, CBAM for the cost and benefit of strategies, and ARID for reviewing an intermediate design. The lightweight methods that teams actually run keep its vocabulary and drop its ceremony: LAAAM scores alternatives against weighted scenarios; TARA is one reviewer on a built system; decision-centric architecture review (DCAR) evaluates decisions against their forces; and LASR, which won a distinguished paper award at ICSA 2026 after 75 reviews, lets two to four developers review their own architecture in half a day against quality goals and a catalogue of standard risks. Simon Brown's risk-storming does the same visually over C4 diagrams.

The draft standard keeps the vocabulary (its finding types are ATAM's) and makes the method a depth: a review answers criteria; an evaluation adds the utility tree and the workshop.

### Continuous and automated assessment

Fitness functions, "an objective integrity assessment of some architectural characteristic", are now the default way to keep a measurable characteristic true after a design is built (Ford, Parsons, Kua and Sadalage, Building Evolutionary Architectures, second edition, 2022, subtitled Automated Software Governance). The tools are mature: ArchUnit and jMolecules for code structure, Structurizr inspections for the model itself, Open Policy Agent for infrastructure and admission policy, and Backstage Tech Insights, Cortex and OpsLevel scorecards for service maturity against a catalogue. The Thoughtworks Technology Radar moved the architecture advice process to Trial in April 2025 and, in April 2026, put "architecture drift reduction with LLMs" at Assess, pairing deterministic rules with model-based checks for semantic violations. DORA's research is the evidence base: heavyweight external change approval "ha[s] a negative impact on software delivery performance" with no evidence that it lowers change failure, and loosely coupled architecture is measured partly by how many design changes need outside approval.

These tools assess conformance to rules, not the quality of a design. They need an accurate catalogue, and scores can be gamed. Their lesson for the continuum is that every criterion a tool can decide should be decided by a tool, on every change, and that people should spend their time on the criteria that need judgement. The draft lenses mark every criterion `tool`, `reviewer` or `both` for that reason.

### Well-Architected reviews

All three cloud frameworks assess a workload: AWS's "collection of resources and code that delivers business value", Azure's "application resources, custom code, AI models, data and supporting infrastructure that together serve business goals". AWS's review is "a constructive conversation, not an audit", its output high-risk and medium-risk issues and an improvement plan, its history kept as milestones; its framework revision is still dated November 2024, and on 1 October 2026 it previewed a Well-Architected Agent that reviews an environment weekly, including infrastructure as code. Azure pairs a questionnaire-based Well-Architected Review with Advisor Score, a continuous score per pillar. Google Cloud has no review tool of its own and instead publishes per-pillar assessment questions as agent skills. On the continuum a workload is what a product runs, so these reviews land on the product and on the reference architecture; their pillars are the core lens's quality areas, and their lenses (generative AI, machine learning, responsible AI, agentic AI) are the evidence behind the AI lenses.

### Maturity

The CNCF Platform Engineering Maturity Model assesses a platform programme on five aspects at four levels, and says that reaching the top level "should not be a goal in itself". CMMI v3.0 assesses an organisational unit's processes. DORA's AI Capabilities Model (September 2025) names seven capabilities that amplify AI-assisted delivery, among them healthy data ecosystems, AI-accessible internal data and quality internal platforms. None of these assesses a design; they are the maturity row of the table above and stay with the capability, the platform and the team.

## Assessing AI solutions

### What changes

An AI solution replaces some deterministic behaviour with learned, probabilistic behaviour supplied by a model the team usually does not own, priced per call, and sensitive to data it did not see at design time. Azure's AI workload guidance names the defining difference as non-determinism and the testing problems as model decay and randomness. For an assessment that means four things: quality scenarios need statistical thresholds rather than exact responses; evals become the test suite and the main evidence; the model and its data are supply-chain dependencies with versions; and the people affected, and the law, have more to say.

| National guidance | Australia's Guidance for AI Adoption (October 2025) and National AI Plan (December 2025); New Zealand's Public Service AI Framework (January 2025); the UK's AI assurance roadmap (September 2025); Singapore's AI Verify | The obligations and practices a design in those jurisdictions names |

### What the AI solution lens adds

The [`ai-solution` lens](../standards/assessment/lenses/ai-solution.yaml) adds 28 criteria in seven areas: whether a model is the right tool and its role bounded; whether the model is pinned, documented and replaceable; whether evals exist with thresholds, gate release, cover the scenarios and populations, and use calibrated judges (LLM-as-judge shows position, verbosity and self-preference bias); whether each classifying eval names its positive class and caps false positives and false negatives separately by what each costs, rather than leaning on F1 or accuracy, whether abstention is measured with unanswerable questions, whether permission trimming is tested as different users with zero tolerance, and whether each threshold rests on enough cases; whether grounding data is owned, permission-trimmed to the caller and monitored for drift; whether untrusted input is contained and model output validated before it reaches a tool, a store or a user; whether calls are bounded in tokens, rate, cost and time and traced (the OpenTelemetry GenAI conventions are still at Development status, so their version must be pinned); and whether oversight is designed, users are told, and the regulatory class and impact assessment are recorded. Each criterion reads the `ai` facet that the design specification puts on any element using a model.

The academic literature agrees on the shape: a 2025 systematic review of 206 sources found 11 quality attributes, 16 tactics and 85 trade-offs specific to ML-enabled systems, and an ICSE 2025 study of 100 LLM and retrieval applications found integration defects of more than three kinds in 77 of them. The defects are in the integration, which is to say in the architecture.

## Assessing AI agents

### What changes

An agent is a model that chooses its own steps, calls tools, keeps memory and acts. Every risk of an AI solution remains, and three questions are added: what can it do, who or what bounds that, and can content it reads change what it does? The sources converge on the answers more closely than one might expect.

### What the agent lens adds

The [`ai-agent` lens](../standards/assessment/lenses/ai-agent.yaml) adds 23 criteria in eight areas, reading the `agent` facet of an element. Several of them are blockers by default, because they are the ones whose failure is an incident rather than a defect: an autonomy level disproportionate to the reversibility of the agent's actions; an incomplete inventory of actions; an irreversible, financial, destructive or outbound action that is neither approved by a person nor bounded by a T0 or T1 limit; a T0 or T1 limit the agent can edit; generated code run outside a sandbox; any session that holds all three legs of the trifecta; an agent without an identity of its own; unauthenticated agent-to-agent traffic; and no step, cost and time limit or kill switch. The rest ask for injection-resistant patterns, scoped credentials, memory isolation and poisoning controls, containment of cascading failure, pinned tools and protocol versions, trajectory logging, protection of human approval from manipulation and automation bias, reconciliation of what the agent did against what policy intended, and evaluation of trajectories and repeated runs.

Much of this is the bundle's own agent-native standard made assessable. The guardrail tiers T0 to T3 and the rule that an agent cannot change its own limits come from the [operating model](../standards/agent-native/operating-model.md); provenance on every action from the [provenance](../standards/agent-native/provenance.md) standard; drift reconciliation from [reconciliation](../standards/agent-native/reconciliation.md); the profile fields from the [agent specification](../standards/agent-native/agent-specification.md). `/architecture runtime-agent` writes an agent that should pass the lens; the lens is how someone other than its author finds out whether it does.

## Assessing a solution before production

Everything above assesses a design. A solution going into production needs one more, formal, judgement: is what was built the design that was assessed, and can it be run? Three traditions answer it. TOGAF's compliance review, run in Phase G, is exactly this comparison of an implementation with its architecture, and its six levels of conformance were made for it. Google's SRE practice has a Production Readiness Review before an SRE team takes on a service, covering architecture and dependencies, instrumentation and monitoring, emergency response, capacity, change management and performance. AWS's Operational Readiness Reviews turn the causes of its own past incidents into questions teams answer before launch and throughout a service's life, as a complement to Well-Architected reviews.

The [draft standard](../standards/assessment/standard-architecture-assessment.md#81-the-production-entry-assessment) takes all three. A production entry assessment is a review, never only a check, of a solution design at the version being released, with an independent lead assessor whatever the rule for other reviews, and an approval by the accountable service owner recorded in the assessment. It brings in a [`production` lens](../standards/assessment/lenses/production.yaml) of 19 criteria that read evidence of the build: an as-built record against this version of the design, rollback, recovery, load and acceptance tests, an operational readiness review, a named support team with alerting on its SLOs, residual risk accepted by an entitled owner, gating evals passing on the release candidate, and, for an agent, a test showing its limits and kill switch work without its cooperation. The record adds `as_built`, TOGAF's conformance scale applied to the built solution against its design; only full conformance, or conformance with every extra feature recorded as a decision, can be endorsed.

The cybersecurity, privacy, AI impact and conformity assessments are not lenses and do not become lenses here. Each reads a different object, follows its own method, often needs an accredited or legally accountable assessor, and ends in an acceptance only its owner can give. The production entry assessment requires that each one the design needs has returned an outcome for the version being released, treats a failed outcome as a blocker, and checks that the residual risks they report are accepted by whoever they name.

## Choosing the assessment: triage

The same method applied with the same weight to everything fails both ways: a knowledge assistant answering staff questions from a few curated documents is buried, and an agent reading inbound email and paying refunds is under-examined. Three sources show how to scale an assessment to the subject. FIPS 199 categorises a system by the highest impact any one of its objectives carries, the high-water mark, and lets management raise the result but not lower it. IMDA's framework for agentic AI asks organisations to assess and bound an agent's risks up front by its action space and its autonomy. AWS's Agentic AI Security Scoping Matrix places agents in four scopes by how far they act without a person (no agency, prescribed, supervised, full) and maps the controls each needs. The EU AI Act's risk classes do the same for effect on people.

The [Assessment Triage Standard](../standards/assessment/standard-assessment-triage.md) combines them into a front door. A few questions answered before an assessment starts fix the assessment type, from what is being assessed (a new component, a platform, a design, a solution, an AI solution, an agent, a change, or entry to production), and a risk tier from AT1 Light to AT4 Critical, the highest that any one driver gives. For agents the main driver is a grid of reach (knowledge, read, write, external, consequential) against agency; the others are data, exposure, untrusted content, effect on people, novelty and reversibility. The tier sets depth, independence, evidence, hand-overs, approver, the route into production and how often the subject is assessed again. Two further rules keep it proportionate without making it soft: a tier is derived again from the finished specification and the derived tier wins, and a subject running inside an assessed platform's envelope inherits the controls the platform already answered, which lowers the effort of its assessment but never its tier. A component new to the landscape has an onboarding assessment of its own, which records the terms on which later designs may use it and takes three of the remaining G253 gaps with it.

## The proposal

Working backwards from a skill that assesses a design, the skill needs a design it can read, criteria it can apply, and a record it can write. Those are the three parts of the [draft standard](../standards/assessment/standard-architecture-assessment.md).

| Part | File | What it settles |
|---|---|---|
| The method | [`standard-architecture-assessment.md`](../standards/assessment/standard-architecture-assessment.md) | Depths, process, results, dispositions, verdict rules, the stage gates, the line with cybersecurity |
| The design specification | [`design.schema.json`](../standards/assessment/schemas/design.schema.json) | A design as YAML: its id (AP, RA or the proposed SD), its place on the continuum by catalogue identifiers, drivers and obligations, trust zones, data, elements typed by continuum kinds, connectors with contracts and trust, functional, quality, failure and misuse scenarios, fitness functions, decisions, considerations, risks, operations and evidence; `ai` and `agent` facets on elements |
| The criteria | [`lens.schema.json`](../standards/assessment/schemas/lens.schema.json) and the [`core`](../standards/assessment/lenses/core.yaml), [`ai-solution`](../standards/assessment/lenses/ai-solution.yaml) and [`ai-agent`](../standards/assessment/lenses/ai-agent.yaml) lenses, and the [`production`](../standards/assessment/lenses/production.yaml) lens | 121 criteria, each with what it reads, whether a tool or a reviewer decides it, a default severity and its sources; the AI lenses are brought in by profiles the design declares and a tool derives, the production lens by a production entry assessment |
| The record | [`assessment.schema.json`](../standards/assessment/schemas/assessment.schema.json) | Subject by id, version and digest; depth; lenses and versions; independent assessors; findings with ATAM's types; dispositions to considerations, decision records and technical debt; risk themes; hand-overs; a verdict with TOGAF's conformance levels |
| The author's guide | [`design-author-guide.md`](design-author-guide.md) | What the author of a design writes, checks and clears before a review, and what each disposition asks of them afterwards |
| The example | [SD-001](../standards/assessment/examples/SD-001-delivery-exception-agent.design.yaml) and [AS-001](../standards/assessment/examples/AS-001-delivery-exception-agent.assessment.yaml) | The delivery-exception agent from `/architecture runtime-agent`, specified with deliberate gaps, and reviewed against all three lenses: not endorsed, because its customer email is generated after untrusted carrier text is in context and is bounded only by a limit the agent holds |

The choices behind it, and the alternatives set aside:

| Choice | Why | Set aside |
|---|---|---|
| One method, with lenses | The questions every design must answer do not change with its technology; what changes is the further questions. A lens per profile keeps the core stable and lets lenses version on their own | A separate review process for AI; a single checklist that grows with every technology |
| AI and agents as facets, not kinds | The continuum and the agent-native standards already say a runtime agent is a service and a model is a technology; new kinds would add levels with nothing of their own to say | A `model` kind and an `agent` kind on the design |
| The specification as data, typed by continuum kinds | A tool can decide 53 of the 121 criteria outright, and whether the answer is present for 39 more, on every change; the kinds let the specification cite the catalogue rather than copy it | Assessing the Markdown document directly; a free-form YAML |
| Findings go to existing kinds | A consideration, a decision record and a debt item already carry the continuum's lifecycle and the ladder already reads them; an assessment backlog would be a second, unlinked one | A findings register of its own |
| Three depths | Check on every change is cheap; review at the gates; evaluation for what is costly to reverse. This is where practice has gone | One depth, either too heavy to run often or too light to trust |
| TOGAF's conformance levels kept, beside a separate outcome | Architects know them, and conformance and fitness are different questions | Dropping them; using them as the verdict |

### From here to a skill

1. Settle the open decisions below, and add the agreed identifier series to the envelope and the frontmatter standard.
2. Implement the tool criteria as a check, in the zero-dependency style of the bundle's validators, reading a specification and the lenses and writing an assessment record with every tool result. This is usable on its own, in CI.
3. Map the `pattern` skill's model to the specification's elements, connectors and scenarios, so that a pattern document can produce its specification and the two are kept in step.
4. Write the `/architecture assess` flow: it runs the check, walks a reviewer (a person or an agent) through the remaining criteria with the design's evidence, writes the record, and opens the considerations, decision records and debt items the dispositions call for. At evaluation depth it facilitates the utility tree. A first version is now in the `architecture` skill (version 0.10.0) as four flows an agent or a person can run on their own: `/architecture specify` turns a design document into a specification, `/architecture triage` sets the type and level of review, `/architecture assess` writes the assessment record, and `/architecture report` turns the record into a plain-language report and slide readout. The skill carries the assessment standards, lenses and schemas as its own copy, and applies the tool criteria by reading their rules, until step 2 replaces that reading with code.
5. Wire the record into `/architecture stage`, so that an endorsed assessment whose digest matches the design is accepted as the reviewer half of a stage's check, and a changed design loses it.
6. Add lenses as the need appears: data platforms, regulated industries, sovereign hosting, readiness.

## Testing the method

The method was tested end to end on a real design, an AI assistant answering staff questions from a knowledge base, in five runs: four from specifications written directly, and one starting from a solution design document written from the pattern skill's template. Each run found faults in the method, all since fixed: the outcome rules applied in order, the line between missing evidence that defers and missing evidence that is a finding, separate tool and reviewer results for one criterion, error costs and sample sizes for evals, target-state positions attested by the responsible architect, including `not-target` and `contained`, exceptions approved through escalation, and the specify flow's template mode.

## Alignment with current practice

The draft standard was checked, practice by practice, against what the research above found to be current. It is aligned on substance: what it asks of a design, the vocabulary of its findings, and its split between tool and reviewer. It departs in four places. Three of them are about how much ceremony surrounds a review, which is exactly where practice has moved furthest. The fourth is that its continuous half does not exist yet.

| Practice | Where it comes from | In the standard | Alignment |
|---|---|---|---|
| Judge a design against its stakeholders' concerns, with a planned evaluation and a report | ISO/IEC/IEEE 42030 and 42020 | Drivers and stakeholders as blocker criteria; the eight steps of section 4 follow 42020 clause 9; the record is the evaluation report | Aligned |
| Quality attributes as measurable scenarios, prioritised; risks, non-risks, sensitivity and trade-off points | ATAM and its lightweight family | Scenario fields in the schema; `CORE-QA-01` to `05`; ATAM's finding types; the utility tree at evaluation depth | Aligned |
| Decisions recorded with alternatives, and reviewed as decisions | ADRs, DCAR | `CORE-DEC-01` to `05`; `record-decision` dispositions | Aligned |
| Threat modelling done with the design, controls assessed separately | Threat Modeling Manifesto, NIST SP 800-160, the security pillars | `CORE-SEC-04`; section 9's hand-over | Aligned |
| Lightweight, proportionate review instead of a heavyweight board | LASR, risk-storming, the advice process, DORA on change approval | Three depths; LASR, risk-storming and DCAR accepted as ways of doing a review | Aligned on depth. Departs on independence: a review needs an assessor who neither wrote nor delivers the design, where LASR and the advice process have the team review its own work and seek advice rather than approval |
| A conversation that ends in an improvement plan, not an audit that ends in a pass or fail | AWS Well-Architected reviews; ATAM, which gives risks and no verdict | Findings, dispositions and an improvement path through considerations, decisions and debt; but also an `endorsed` or `not-endorsed` outcome | Departs, deliberately: the verdict exists because the definition ladder needs something to gate on. Section 7 keeps it apart from conformance, and an `accept-risk` or `accept-as-debt` disposition keeps most findings out of it |
| Every decidable criterion decided by a tool, on every change | Fitness functions, policy and architecture rules as code, service scorecards, the Well-Architected Agent | `decided_by: tool` on 53 of 121 criteria; the check depth; `CORE-EVO-01` asks the design for its own fitness functions | Aligned in design, not yet in practice: the tool criteria are specified and not implemented, so until step 2 above is done every check is a reading by hand |
| Agents as assessors, with a person accountable | AWS Well-Architected Agent; Google's assessment skills; Thoughtworks on LLM-assisted drift reduction | An agent may assess with provenance; a person makes the verdict (A5) | Aligned |
| AI judged by evals and agents by what they can do | NIST AI 600-1, OWASP's two lists, the AWS and Azure AI guidance, IMDA | The AI solution and agent lenses | Aligned, with the three W260 candidates and the six G253 gaps above as the next criteria |
| Assessment scaled to risk, decided before it starts | FIPS 199's high-water mark; IMDA on bounding agents by action space and autonomy; AWS's Agentic AI Security Scoping Matrix; the EU AI Act's risk classes | The triage standard: assessment types, tiers AT1 to AT4, reach and agency, platform inheritance | Aligned. It also answers A7: at AT1 the team assesses itself, and an independent assessor joins from AT2 |
| A formal readiness review before production, fed by incident learning | TOGAF Phase G compliance review; Google SRE's Production Readiness Review; AWS Operational Readiness Reviews | The production entry assessment (section 8.1) and the `production` lens; the `incident` trigger turns uncovered incident causes into criteria | Aligned. It reads the outcomes of the cybersecurity, privacy, AI impact and conformity assessments rather than standing in for them |
| Help for the author before the review, so the review spends its time on judgement | Well-Architected's self-service review; LASR's preparation; the advice process's expectation that the decider seeks advice early | Not in the standard | Was missing; now the [design author's guide](design-author-guide.md) |
| The assessment practice measured, so that it can be lightened or tightened on evidence | DORA's capability measures; the advice process's reflection on decisions | Not present | Departs: nothing records how long assessments take, which findings recur, or whether incidents trace back to risks that were found or missed |

Two of the departures are choices for the owner, added below as A7 and A8. The other two need work rather than a decision: implementing the tool criteria, and recording the few measures that would tell whether the assessments are worth what they cost (time from request to verdict, findings per criterion across designs, and incidents traced to a finding or to its absence).

Standard 0.8.0 answers the weight with section 3.1, Keeping it light: an advice process by default up to AT2, people answering only the criteria the tier asks for, change assessments limited to what changed, a one-page record up to AT2, time-boxed sessions, and a tool that applies every one of the 92 tool-decided and shared criteria. The anchors do not lighten: triage, blockers, approved exceptions, a person confirming, and production evidence.

## Decisions for the owner

| # | Decision | Options | Recommended |
|---|---|---|---|
| A1 | Identifier for a solution design | `SD-NNN`; reuse `RA-NNN` for instances; no identifier, by file name | `SD-NNN`. A solution design instantiates a reference architecture for one consumer and is assessed on its own; it needs to be citable |
| A2 | Identifier and home for an assessment record | `AS-NNN` beside the design; a register only; a section of the design | `AS-NNN`, one file per assessment beside the design, so a design's history of assessments is its folder |
| A3 | Is the design specification a new kind on the continuum? | A kind with its own envelope; the data of a pattern or design document, not a kind | Not a kind. It is the machine form of a document the continuum already has, linked by `design.source` |
| A4 | Where the lenses live | In this bundle's standards; in a bundle of their own; in each workspace | In this bundle, under `standards/assessment/lenses/`, with workspaces able to add lenses of their own through a binding |
| A5 | Who may make a verdict | A person only; a person or an agent; an agent with a person's countersignature for blockers | A person, on findings that an agent may make. The agent-native principle that the human budget is spent on the irreversible applies: a verdict is what lets a design be built |
| A6 | Whether an endorsed assessment is required at R3 and R4 | Required; optional evidence | Required at R4, optional at R3, until the tool exists to make the R3 review cheap |
| A7 | Whether every review needs an independent assessor | Always; only at evaluation depth and for a design whose decisions include a one-way door; never, with the team reviewing itself as in LASR | Settled by triage, if the triage standard is accepted: the team assesses itself at AT1, and an independent assessor is required from AT2. A one-way-door decision makes a subject at least AT3 |
| A8 | Whether the outcome is a pass or fail verdict | Keep the four outcomes; findings and an improvement plan only, with the ladder reading open blockers directly | Keep the outcomes, since the ladder needs one, but present the record findings-first, so the outcome reads as a summary of the dispositions rather than a mark |

## Caveats

Each research finding was marked as verified on a primary source, verified only on secondary sources, or unverified, and the document above uses only the first two. The main limits:

- The Open Group's online texts require a login. On 28 October 2026 the TOGAF chapters, G203 and O-AA 2.0 were read online while signed in, and G253, Technical Corrigendum 1 (U254) and W260 from their PDFs. ArchiMate 4 (C260), G20F, G210, G268 and the Digital Practitioner Body of Knowledge were not read in full; what this page says of them rests on the official catalogue and secondary sources.
- iso.org returned errors throughout; ISO statuses and contents come from publishers' official preview PDFs and national standards bodies' listings.
- The EU Digital Omnibus (Regulation (EU) 2026/1744) and the decision citing EN 18286 were confirmed through the Commission's AI Act Service Desk timeline and secondary legal sources, not EUR-Lex itself.
- On 20 October 2026 NIST's project page for the AI control overlays recorded a rename from "Artificial Intelligence" to "Super Intelligence" (COSAiS to COSIS), following a United States executive order of 29 September 2026, and CISA's AI pages changed their terms the same way. The documents themselves are unaltered; this page uses the names under which they were published.
- Fast-moving specifications (MCP, A2A, the OpenTelemetry GenAI conventions, the OWASP lists, MITRE ATLAS) were checked at the versions named and should be pinned, not assumed, by any lens criterion that cites them.
